THE SIMULATED SUN

The Sun this site shows by default is a simulation: a computer-generated
model, made for this site in October 2026. It is not a photograph, not an
observation, and not a record of any real day on the Sun. The features on it
do not exist.

The site can also show the NASA footage the model was calibrated against.
The label beside the Sun says which one the page is showing; this note
describes the simulation.

WHAT IT IMITATES

NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) photographs the Sun in extreme
ultraviolet light. Its 304 ångström channel shows the layer just above the
visible surface, where the plasma is at roughly 50,000 kelvin, and NASA
colors those images red-orange. The simulation imitates that view and uses
the same coloring.

HOW IT WAS MADE

1. A magnetic network. Magnetic flux concentrations are scattered along the
   edges of convection cells about 30,000 km across, as on the real Sun.
   Twenty active regions are added (bright patches, with ribbons either side
   of the line where the magnetic polarity changes and a dark filament along
   it), ten on each half of the sphere, and ten dark filaments in the quiet
   areas. They are placed by hand along two belts north and south of the
   equator, so that the turning Sun shows a different face as it goes round;
   the arrangement is the model's own and follows no observation.

2. Turbulence, combed along a direction. Random noise is added at every
   scale from one pixel (about 900 km) upward, and its finest scales are
   stretched along a direction that is random over the quiet Sun and follows
   the polarity lines and the filaments near them. Where the result is strong
   it becomes the bright clumps of the network; where it is weak, the dark
   lanes between them.

3. Calibration against one real frame. In one frame of SDO's 304 Å footage
   from September 29-30, 2013 (NASA Goddard/SDO) the following were measured:
   how much detail there is at each scale and orientation, how sparse and how
   lopsided it is, how detail at different scales and orientations occurs
   together, and how bright the picture is at each height around the edge of
   the disk. The model's map was then adjusted toward those measurements,
   until most of its own, taken over the whole map, agreed with them to
   within a few percent. Only those measurements were taken from the real
   frame: about 22,000 numbers. No pixel of any observation is in the
   picture.

4. A sphere and a camera. The map is a picture of the whole sphere, all the
   way round, and the camera sees one half of it at a time. Over each loop
   the picture turns through the whole map once. Toward the poles the map's
   texture is laid out afresh, so that it has the same grain there as
   everywhere else instead of streaks that close in on each pole. The sphere
   carries a forest of short upright columns, so the texture thickens and
   softens toward the edge, its dark lanes partly fill in there, and it ends
   in a ragged fringe of spicules that grows out of it. Near the edge the
   texture is also drawn out along the radius and kept sharp across it, as
   in the real frame: seen at a slant, the glowing layer is looked through,
   not seen as a thin skin. Beyond the edge are a glow and a few small
   plumes. The glow follows the turn: it is brighter where active ground
   stands at the edge.

5. Motion. The sphere turns once in each loop, about the upright axis
   through its middle, so its features come in at the left edge, cross the
   disk and go out at the right. Besides that, most of the change happens in
   place: small bright features flare and fade, larger clumps swell and dim,
   the fine detail shimmers, and the spicules of the fringe rise and vanish.
   A little of it is slow sideways drift. This change in place repeats every
   eight seconds, twice in each turn, so the slower turn does not slow it.
   Everything repeats a whole number of times per loop, so the last of the
   480 frames leads back into the first without a cut, and each frame
   carries a little fresh grain. (Sixteen seconds at 30 frames a second. The
   compressed video file restarts on its one keyframe, which is slightly
   crisper than the frames before it.)

THE TURN

The real Sun turns once in about twenty-seven days, about an axis that lies
across this view, so its features cross the disk from one side to the
other. The model turns the same way, but once every sixteen seconds: about
150,000 times as fast. That turn is the page's own motion, chosen for the
look of it, not a record of the Sun's: its speed comes from no data, and no
observation is used for any of its pixels. It is drawn into the video
itself; the page's code turns nothing. Pause motion stops it.

WHAT IT IS NOT

It is not a physical simulation of the Sun's plasma: that takes a
supercomputer and still covers only a small patch. It is a statistical and
geometric model, built to look like SDO's view and checked against it. It
predicts nothing.

HOW IT WAS CHECKED

A classifier was trained to tell small patches of one picture from patches
of another by their texture statistics, and scored on patches it had not
seen: 0.5 means it cannot tell them apart, 1.0 that it always can. The
patches come from the middle of the disk, within 0.6 of its radius.

- The model's surface against the frame it was calibrated on: 0.63. A
  finished frame, before video compression: 0.84; the far side of the
  sphere, half a turn later: 0.82. For scale, one picture in two forms
  (a frame before and after compression, or a video's first frame and
  its still) scores 0.63 to 0.72.
- The finished video against the NASA video, like for like: their first
  frames 0.87; frames from the middle of the loop 0.92 to 0.94. Frames
  from the middle of the NASA video score 0.60 to 0.63 against each other,
  and the frame the model was calibrated on scores 0.76 to 0.82 against
  them.

So the model's surface is about as close to the frame it was tuned on as
this test can measure. The finished frames, with the sphere turned, lit and
seen through its forest, are easier to tell from that frame, and further
still from the rest of the real footage, more than real frames are from
each other. The test looks only at those statistics. An expert
would still see what they do not measure: the model turns far faster than
the Sun does, the glow beyond its edge brightens and fades much faster than
the real one's, its active regions lack the fine structure of real ones, and
nothing erupts.

Around the edge of the disk, in eight frames spread evenly over the loop,
the average brightness at each height, from just inside the edge to well
outside it, is within 2.2 levels (of 255) of the real frame's.

THE REAL THING

The NASA footage the model was calibrated against, which this site showed
before the simulation:
Filament Eruption Creates 'Canyon of Fire' on the Sun, SVS 11379.
https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/11379/
Credit for that footage: NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center/SDO.
The site's source code still holds it, and the site can be switched back to
it.

When the simulation is the Sun on show, these are made from its first frame:
the image shown while the video loads, for reduced motion, and after a media
failure; the link-preview image; and the browser icons (square crop around
the disk, black sky converted to transparency at the small sizes). The page
shows the Sun in the window's upper-right corner, at most 200 pixels across,
so it uses the 1280-pixel files only; the 2048-pixel ones stay in the source
code.

The code that generates all of this is in the site's source code, in
scripts/sun/.
